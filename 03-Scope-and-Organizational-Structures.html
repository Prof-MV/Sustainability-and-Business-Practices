3  Project Scope, Charter, and Organizational Structures

3.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  1. Evaluate a candidate project with payback period and net present value (NPV).
  2. Write a project charter and explain what authority its sign-off grants.
  3. Identify the five inputs to a charter and the benefits of using one.
  4. Build a stakeholder register and place stakeholders on a power–interest grid.
  5. Develop a scope statement — deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions, constraints, assumptions — and distinguish product scope from project scope.
  6. Recognize scope creep and run a change-control process.
  7. Compare functional, projectized, and matrix (weak/balanced/strong) structures and the trade-offs each imposes on the project manager.
Before we begin: who told you it was okay to start?

On your last group project, was there a moment where everyone agreed this is what we are building and this is who signed off? Or did work just… start? The charter is that moment, written down.

3.2 Introduction: nothing official without a charter

Your company must get Senior Management to approve the Scope and Charter before work begins on the RC-vehicle replica. That is not bureaucracy — it is the difference between a project and a hobby. “There is no official project without an approved Project Charter.”

Two questions come first: should we do this project at all (selection), and exactly what are we committing to (scope). Then the organization’s shape — functional, projectized, or matrix — decides how much authority you actually have to deliver it.

Note

Industry snapshot. In automotive new-product introduction, a project cannot pass its first gate without a signed charter and a business case showing positive NPV. A food company scoping a new line writes a statement of work that names the throughput, the allergen controls, and what is explicitly out. A defense contractor’s SOW is a contract exhibit — every deliverable and exclusion in it is legally enforceable.

3.3 Project selection: is it worth doing?

3.3.1 Payback period

The simplest screen: how long until the project’s net cash inflows repay the initial outlay?

\[\text{Payback} = \text{years until cumulative cash flow} \ge 0\]

3.3.2 Worked Example 1: payback on the RC-vehicle tooling investment

The project needs $450 000 up front (tooling, setup, approvals). Once the 100 000-unit contract runs, it returns an estimated $180 000 of net cash per year.

initial   <- 450000
annual_cf <- 180000
horizon   <- 5

cum <- cumsum(rep(annual_cf, horizon)) - initial
year_paid <- which(cum >= 0)[1]
# Fractional year within the payback year:
prev <- if (year_paid == 1) -initial else cum[year_paid - 1]
frac <- -prev / annual_cf

cat("Cumulative cash flow by year:", paste0("$", format(cum, big.mark = " ")), "\n")
cat(sprintf("Payback period: %.2f years\n", (year_paid - 1) + frac))
Cumulative cash flow by year: $-270 000 $ -90 000 $  90 000 $ 270 000 $ 450 000 
Payback period: 2.50 years

Payback is quick to compute but ignores the time value of money and anything that happens after the payback point. NPV fixes both.

3.3.3 Net present value

NPV discounts every future cash flow back to today’s dollars at the organization’s required rate of return \(r\), then subtracts the initial outlay. A positive NPV means the project earns more than that hurdle rate.

\[NPV = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t}\]

3.3.4 Try it yourself: NPV of the project

What happens to NPV if the hurdle rate rises to 18 %?

Raise r in the cell and re-run. Higher discount rates punish cash flows that arrive later, so NPV falls — a project that clears a 12 % hurdle may fail an 18 % one. This is why a company with expensive capital (or a risky project that demands a risk premium) is choosier about which projects it starts. Course Learning Outcome 1 is exactly this: communicate the philosophy of project selection using the NPV method.

3.4 The project charter

PMI: developing a document that formally authorizes a project and documents the initial requirements that satisfy the stakeholders’ needs and expectations. Approval of the charter formally initiates the project.

3.4.1 Five inputs to a charter

  1. Project Statement of Work — the high-level “project brief”: business need and product scope, fewer details than the scope statement.
  2. Business Case — why the project is being done (the NPV/payback story).
  3. Contract (if the client is external).
  4. Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEFs) — conditions you cannot control (market, regulation, weather).
  5. Organizational Process Assets (OPAs) — the company’s plans, processes, policies, and accumulated knowledge.

3.4.2 What a charter contains

Title and description; business case; pre-assigned resources; stakeholders and their known requirements; product deliverables and the end result; measurable objectives tied to strategy; a summary milestone schedule; a summary budget (gross only — the real budget comes after planning); approval requirements and who signs off; high-level risks; and signatures.

3.4.3 Why bother

  • Stakeholders are clearly defined
  • Communication channels are defined
  • Roles and responsibilities are outlined
  • Scope is defined — helping prevent scope creep
  • Better project processes in later phases
  • Higher probability of success
  • Buy-in from the team

The charter is a living document: update it as the project moves. Used well, it is “the single most important tool the project manager uses to manage the expectations of the sponsor and all other stakeholders.”

3.5 Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any individual or group with an active interest in the project who can affect it.

Internal External
Senior management, accountant, team members, IT department Client, competitors, suppliers, environmental agencies, government agencies, research labs

Managing them is a loop: identify → gather information → determine strengths and weaknesses → predict behaviour → build an engagement strategy → implement. The point of engagement is to increase support and reduce resistance — by listening, informing, and helping stakeholders along.

3.6 The scope statement

Written early in planning, the scope statement is more detailed than the charter and should include:

  • The project statement (what will be done)
  • Acceptance criteria and the validation/testing plan
  • Deliverables
  • Exclusions (what is explicitly not in scope)
  • Constraints and assumptions
  • Project coordination
  • Signed-off review with the customer and with corporate management

Product scope = the features and functions of the thing you are building. Project scope = the work needed to deliver it. A scope statement covers both, plus boundaries and references to specs. As the project progresses, scope should get clearer and more specific — and changes here should be avoided, though not forbidden.

3.6.1 The scope management processes

Plan scope → collect requirements → define scope → create WBS → validate scope (at closing) → control scope (throughout). The scope management plan is often run as an extension of Management by Objectives (MBO): set unambiguous, realistic objectives; check periodically whether they are being met; take corrective action.

3.7 Scope creep

Scope creep is the uncontrolled growth of requirements over the project’s life:

  • one deliverable quietly becomes five
  • three “essential” features become ten
  • mid-project, the customer’s needs change and requirements are reopened

Its usual causes are stakeholders changing requirements and internal miscommunication. It is not always bad — customer needs genuinely evolve, and refusing all change can deliver the wrong product. The problem is uncontrolled change. A good PM expects it and plans for it with a change-control process: every change request is logged, its impact on scope, schedule, and cost is assessed, and it is approved or rejected before work changes.

3.8 Organizational culture

Every organization develops its own outlook, policies, habits of thought, and norms — “as unique as a fingerprint.” Culture is the solution to problems that has worked consistently for a group and is therefore taught to new members as the correct way to perceive and think. It is shaped by technology, environment, geographic location, reward systems, rules and procedures, key organizational members, and critical incidents.

Culture affects projects through:

  • Department interaction — cooperative cultures run projects far better than adversarial ones.
  • Employee commitment to goals — teams need to understand the why to be motivated.
  • Project planning — some cultures tolerate padding estimates but not being late.
  • Performance evaluation — a culture that rewards innovation breeds aggressive, high-payoff project management (with occasional big losses).

3.9 Organizational structures

How the lines of authority and communication are arranged decides when people are available and how you get them.

org <- data.frame(

Structure Strengths for the PM Weaknesses for the PM
Functional (grouped by specialty; one clear boss; each department runs its own projects) No restructuring needed; deep expertise; everyone knows their place Many layers to reach people; weak customer focus; projects drag because staff have “day jobs” and other priorities
Projectized (the company just does projects; team co-located) One reporting line; easy communication; fast decisions; strong PMs; rapid market response Teams are expensive to set up and maintain; loyalty to the project over the organization; hard to keep a shared pool of expertise; team anxiety about “what next” when the project ends
Matrix (blend; classified weak / balanced / strong by PM influence) Suited to dynamic environments; values both project and functional efficiency; coordinates across units; maximizes scarce shared resources Two bosses; heavy time spent negotiating shared resources; frustrating for staff caught between project and functional demands
Which structure is the RC-vehicle project running in?

Almost certainly a matrix — the company has an existing manufacturing line and functional departments (design, quality, purchasing), and it borrows people into the project. That means the PM shares the design engineers with their functional manager and must negotiate their time. Expect the “administrative conflict” of Chapter 10: every matrix team member has two bosses.

3.10 Key Takeaways

  • Select projects on merit: payback is quick but ignores the time value of money; NPV discounts every future cash flow and is the course’s standard (CLO 1). Positive NPV = beats the hurdle rate.
  • The charter formally initiates the project. Inputs: SOW, business case, contract, EEFs, OPAs. It defines stakeholders, roles, and scope, and earns team buy-in. No approved charter, no official project.
  • Manage stakeholders on a power–interest grid: manage the high-power/high-interest closely, monitor the rest.
  • The scope statement covers product and project scope, plus acceptance criteria, exclusions, constraints, and assumptions, and is signed off.
  • Scope creep is uncontrolled requirement growth. Change is normal; uncontrolled change is the enemy — run every request through change control for a cost/schedule impact and a decision.
  • Culture shapes project outcomes through department cooperation, commitment, planning honesty, and what performance evaluation rewards.
  • Structure sets the PM’s real authority: near zero in functional, near total in projectized, negotiated in the matrix (weak/balanced/strong) — where everyone has two bosses.

3.11 Practice Problems

Problem 1: Payback vs. NPV disagree

Project A: $300 000 outlay, then $150 000/yr for 3 years, nothing after. Project B: $300 000 outlay, then $90 000/yr for 6 years. Hurdle rate 10 %. Which has the shorter payback, and which has the higher NPV?

Solution.

npv <- function(c0, cfs, r) -c0 + sum(cfs / (1 + r)^seq_along(cfs))
A <- c(150000, 150000, 150000)
B <- rep(90000, 6)
cat(sprintf("A: payback 2.0 yr, NPV $%s\n", format(round(npv(3e5, A, 0.10)), big.mark = " ")))
cat(sprintf("B: payback ~3.3 yr, NPV $%s\n", format(round(npv(3e5, B, 0.10)), big.mark = " ")))
A: payback 2.0 yr, NPV $73 028
B: payback ~3.3 yr, NPV $91 973

A pays back faster; B has the higher NPV because its cash flows keep coming for three more years. Payback alone would pick the worse project.

Problem 2: Charter or scope statement?

Which document does each belong in?

  1. “Gross budget: approximately $650 000.” → Charter (summary budget).
  2. “Body colourways: Racing Red and Gunmetal only; no custom colours.” → Scope statement (exclusions).
  3. “Sponsor: VP Operations. Signs off at Gate 1.” → Charter (approval/signatures).
  4. “Drop test: survives 10 drops from 1.5 m onto concrete, no functional damage.” → Scope statement (acceptance criteria).
Problem 3 (discussion): the functional manager won’t release the good engineer

In a weak matrix, your best-choice design engineer’s functional manager keeps assigning them to department work and offers you a junior instead. Using the culture and structure ideas in this chapter, explain why this happens and what you can do.

Discussion points. The functional manager is evaluated on their department’s output, so they hoard their strongest people — a reward-system effect. In a weak matrix you have little formal authority to override it. Levers: escalate to the sponsor (whose NPV depends on the project), negotiate a part-time or time-boxed assignment, trade a deliverable the functional manager cares about, or get the resource commitment written into the charter so it is a documented agreement rather than a favour.
