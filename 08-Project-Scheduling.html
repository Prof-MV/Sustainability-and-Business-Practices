8  Project Scheduling

8.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  1. Read and build a Gantt chart and explain what it adds over a network diagram.
  2. Distinguish the four precedence relationships (FS, FF, SS, SF) and use leads and lags.
  3. Combine PERT activity estimates into a project mean and standard deviation.
  4. Calculate the probability of finishing by a target date.
  5. Compute an activity’s crash cost slope and build a least-cost crashing sequence.
  6. Compare crashing and fast-tracking, and state the risks of each.
Before we begin: a schedule is a promise — how confident are you in it?

“The project finishes in 83 days” sounds precise. Is that a date you would bet your bonus on, or a coin flip? This chapter turns a single-number schedule into a probability you can actually stand behind.

8.2 Introduction: from a network to a calendar

The AON network from Chapter 6 tells you the project takes N days. The schedule puts those days on a calendar, assigns resources, and gives you something to track progress against. The classic tool is the Gantt chart, developed by Henry Gantt between 1910 and 1915 and used at scale for the first time on the Hoover Dam (1931–1936).

Note

Industry snapshot. An automotive program’s master schedule is a Gantt with thousands of rows rolled up to a one-page milestone view for the steering committee. A food-plant install schedule shows the trades stacked so the project manager can see where two crews need the same space on the same day. A defense schedule is baselined and every slip against it is reported.

8.3 Gantt charts

A Gantt chart is a time-phased network: it links each activity to a calendar. Linked to a schedule, it also tracks progress against the plan. Benefits: easy to read; shows the network and the schedule together; supports updating and control; useful for assigning resources; and easy to create.

8.4 Precedence relationships

Most dependencies are finish-to-start, but there are four:

Relationship Meaning Example
Finish-to-Start (FS) B can’t start until A finishes Build the wall before you paint it
Finish-to-Finish (FF) B can’t finish until A finishes Can’t finish editing until writing is finished
Start-to-Start (SS) B can’t start until A starts (often simultaneous) Start trimming edges when you start mowing
Start-to-Finish (SF) A starting triggers B finishing (rare) The new shift starting ends the old shift

A lead lets a successor start early (overlap); a lag forces a wait (e.g. “wait 2 days for the paint to cure” is FS + 2-day lag).

8.5 Probabilistic scheduling with PERT

A single duration hides uncertainty. Using three-point estimates (Chapter 6), for each activity:

\[t_e = \frac{o + 4m + p}{6}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{p - o}{6}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \left(\frac{p - o}{6}\right)^2\]

For the project, sum along the critical path:

\[\mu_{\text{project}} = \sum t_e, \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{project}} = \sum \sigma^2, \qquad \sigma_{\text{project}} = \sqrt{\sigma^2_{\text{project}}}\]

8.5.2 Try it yourself: probability of hitting a target date

Standardize the target: \(z = \dfrac{\text{target} - \mu}{\sigma}\), then read the normal cumulative probability.

Why quote “day 86 at 90 % confidence” instead of “day 83”?

Day 83 is the mean — by definition you have only about a 50 % chance of meeting it. Committing to the mean is committing to a coin flip. Adding a buffer to reach 85–90 % confidence is how you turn a schedule into a promise you can keep, and it is the honest version of the “padding” problem from Chapter 9 — one visible buffer at the project level, not hidden padding in every task.

8.6 Crashing the schedule

When you crash: the initial schedule was too optimistic; the market wants the product sooner; the project has slipped badly; or the contract rewards finishing early / punishes finishing late.

How you accelerate an activity: improve the productivity of existing resources (focus the team, review best practice); change the working method (swap drawing review for CAD model review); or add resources (overtime, extra shifts, expedited freight).

Warning

Caution on overtime. Research on engineering work found optimal marginal output at about 4 hours of overtime per week; by 10 hours of overtime the marginal extra output had fallen to zero. More hours is not more work.

Every activity has a crash cost slope — the cost to buy back one time unit:

\[\text{slope} = \frac{\text{crash cost} - \text{normal cost}}{\text{normal time} - \text{crash time}}\]

8.6.1 Try it yourself: least-cost crashing

To shorten the project, crash critical-path activities in cheapest-slope- first order, one time unit at a time, until you hit the target or run out of crashable critical tasks.

8.7 Fast-tracking versus crashing

Crashing Fast-tracking
Method Add resources (overtime, staff, expediting) Overlap tasks that were sequential
Cost Always adds cost Little or no added cost
Risk Diminishing returns; overtime burnout Rework if the predecessor changes after the successor started

Both only help on the critical path, and after either one you must re-check the network — the critical path can move.

8.8 Key Takeaways

  • A Gantt chart puts the network on a calendar and lets you track progress; it shows activities, dependencies, and resources at once.
  • Four precedence types: FS (default), FF, SS, SF — with leads (overlap) and lags (forced wait).
  • PERT project: \(\mu = \sum t_e\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{\sum \sigma^2}\) along the critical path. The mean is a ~50 % date.
  • Probability of on-time: \(z = (\text{target} - \mu)/\sigma\), then the normal CDF. Commit to an ~85–90 % date, not the mean.
  • Crash cost slope \(=\) (crash cost − normal cost) / (normal time − crash time). Crash critical tasks, cheapest slope first.
  • Crashing buys time with money; fast-tracking buys it with rework risk. Re-check the critical path after either.

8.9 Case Study: Meridian R/C — Week 8

Tip📁 CASE STUDY FILE — Week 8: Can the Schedule Be Pulled In?

This week you pull up the Gantt chart derived from the fixed CPM network for the Trailblazer prototype — 10 activities, 67 working days, critical path A–B–E–F–H–I–J — and the picture is uncomfortably tight. Every activity on that spine has zero float: concept CAD, detailed design release, motor & controls procurement (E, 25 days), prototype assembly (F), toy-safety certification (H, 12 days), packaging & client sample ship, and your sign-off report to Renata Ford. The only slack anywhere in the network sits in body tooling (C, 5 days float), frame tooling (D, 10 days float), and the drop/durability test (G, 7 days float) — none of which help you finish sooner.

Daniel Cho, sitting in on the Friday steering call, asks the question you knew was coming: with Kestrel’s 100,000-unit holiday-season order riding on this prototype, can the 67-day timeline be pulled in? You walk the team through the two realistic levers, both of which only work because E and H — and nothing else in the network — are actually critical.

Crashing E (motor & controls procurement) buys up to 5 days, at $1,200 per day for air freight and expedited customs — a maximum of $6,000 to shave the full 5 days off the 25-day activity.

Crashing H (toy-safety certification) buys up to 4 days, at $900 per day for priority lab review — a maximum of $3,600 to shave the full 4 days off the 12-day activity.

There is also a fast-tracking option on H: instead of waiting for F (assembly) to finish before booking the certification lab, Sofia Reyes could pre-book the lab slot while assembly is still underway. That saves roughly 3 days at little direct cost — but if a late change during assembly affects the sample, the pre-booked test may have to be repeated, eating into the time it was meant to save.

Laying the numbers side by side for Daniel, one thing jumps out: dollar for dollar, H is actually the cheaper activity to crash ($900/day versus E’s $1,200/day), even though E offers more total days of compression (5 versus 4). Applying the chapter’s own cheapest-slope-first rule, H is the activity to crash first if only a few days are needed; E is the fallback if more days are required than H alone can supply. Whichever lever gets pulled, the team agrees to re-check the critical path afterward — crashing or fast-tracking either activity could shift where the true bottleneck sits.

8.10 Practice Problems

Problem 1: Least-cost crash to a deadline

Project critical path A–F–H, currently 23 weeks; the contract needs 20. Crash data: A $513/wk (max 4 wk), F $1 500/wk (max 1 wk), H $950/wk (max 2 wk). Find the least-cost way to remove 3 weeks.

Solution.

crash <- data.frame(
  id = c("A", "F", "H"),
  slope = c(513, 1500, 950),
  max = c(4, 1, 2)
)
need <- 3
crash <- crash[order(crash$slope), ]
left <- need; cost <- 0
for (i in seq_len(nrow(crash))) {
  take <- min(left, crash$max[i]); if (take <= 0) break
  cost <- cost + take * crash$slope[i]; left <- left - take
  cat(sprintf("Crash %s by %d wk @ $%d = $%s\n", crash$id[i], take, crash$slope[i],
              format(take * crash$slope[i], big.mark = " ")))
}
cat(sprintf("Total: $%s for %d weeks\n", format(cost, big.mark = " "), need - left))
Crash A by 3 wk @ $513 = $1 539
Total: $1 539 for 3 weeks

Crash A by 3 weeks for $1 539 — far cheaper than touching F or H.

Problem 2: Confidence in a date

A project’s critical path has \(\mu = 60\) days and \(\sigma = 4\) days. The customer wants delivery in 64 days.

  1. What is the probability of meeting day 64? (b) What date should you commit to for 95 % confidence?

Solution.

mu <- 60; sigma <- 4
cat(sprintf("P(finish by day 64) = %.1f%%\n", 100 * pnorm((64 - mu) / sigma)))
cat(sprintf("95%% confident date  = day %.1f\n", mu + qnorm(0.95) * sigma))
P(finish by day 64) = 84.1%
95% confident date  = day 66.6
  1. ~84 %. (b) Day 66.6 — commit to day 67.
Problem 3 (discussion): crash or fast-track the RC-vehicle launch?

The retailer moves the launch three weeks earlier. Design is on the critical path and is followed by prototype assembly (also critical). You could add a second designer and pay overtime (crash), or start building the frame jig from the released frame drawings while the body design is still being finalized (fast-track). Which, and why?

Discussion points. Fast-tracking the jig against the frame drawings is low-cost and reasonably safe if the frame design is stable — but if the body design forces a late frame change, the jig is rework. Crashing design with a second modeller adds cost and has coordination overhead (two people on one model), but no rework risk. A common answer: fast-track the clearly stable parts, crash the rest, and hold a contingency for the rework risk you accepted.
