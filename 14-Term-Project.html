14  Term Project: Instructions and Templates

This chapter is the home of the MGMT-3105 term project. It holds the project description, the instructions for each phase, the due dates, and the templates you download and fill in. Check back here whenever you start a new phase.

14.1 Overview

You and your group will plan a project the way a real project team would, using the tools from the earlier chapters. The project is planned, not built: you produce the planning documents a company would need before it commits to the work.

Phase Focus Grade weight
A Project Scope, Project Charter, Work Breakdown Structure 10 %
B Corrected Phase A material, AON diagram, Risks, Gantt chart 10 %
C Costs analysis and costs chart 6 %
Important

Deadlines are set by the FOL submission folders for each phase. Late submissions lose 15 % per day. Plan to finish early.

14.2 Groups

  • Groups have 3 students. Self-enrol in a group on FanshaweOnline (FOL). Self-enrolment closes on the date shown on the FOL group sign-up page.
  • Anyone who has not enrolled by then is placed in a group at random.
  • Each group has a discussion board and a locker on FOL for exchanging files and keeping backups.
  • Once your group is formed, create a company name and logo that fit your project. Put them on every document you submit.

14.3 Roles and evaluation surveys

  • Each group has one Project Manager (PM) at a time. Each phase has a different PM.
  • The PM submits the group report for the phase. Submit it digitally through the Phase submission folder on FOL.
  • Every phase also has an evaluation survey on FOL. There is a separate survey for each phase, and each phase has a PM version and a Team Member version. Fill out the version that matches your role in that phase.
  • The project is a team effort. A member who does not contribute receives no marks for the group submission.
Important

The survey is a grading gate. Completing your survey for a phase is required for your grade on that phase’s submission folder. If you miss the survey, your grade for that phase’s submission is 0. This applies to each student individually. The survey due dates are set in FOL.

14.3.1 Why the self-evaluation matters

Take the self-evaluation seriously. It is how you reflect on what you contributed, what worked, and what you would change, and it is the record of how your group ran the phase. The survey answers do not change your grade. Only the instructor assigns grades in this course, and no student’s survey answers decide another student’s grade.

14.3.2 If your team has a problem

If a team member is not pulling their weight, the survey alone is not enough. The team must do both of the following:

  1. Report the issue in the survey.
  2. Tell the instructor directly, by email or in a face-to-face conversation.

Raise problems early, while there is still time to fix them.

  • A cover sheet, following the provided template.
  • Content that is easy to read and understand.
  • Diagrams large enough to read their text. A large diagram may be split into parts if it stays easy to follow.

14.4 The project description

The shaded box below is the background for the project: the situation your company is in. Everything outside the box, in the phase sections that follow, is an instruction about what to hand in.

Tip📁 PROJECT DESCRIPTION — Remote-Controlled Vehicle Replica

Your company specializes in manufacturing remote-controlled vehicles for the toy industry. Because your products are lifelike replicas of real-world vehicles, you have been selected to supply a Project Management Package and to manage a project that will create one new vehicle replica for the coming holiday season.

If selected, your company has the discretion to choose an aircraft (civilian or military), a yacht, or a road vehicle (sport, ATV, or RV type). Your company designs the outer body style and the inner frame that supports the motor, drive, and control systems, at an approximate scale of 1/18, or 9–12 inches (24–30 cm). The body can be made from steel, aluminum, or carbon fibre. The inner frame is either aluminum or carbon fibre.

You will purchase the motor and control systems from one of two Canadian suppliers. If your replica is accepted and signed off by the client, your company is awarded a contract to manufacture 100,000+ replicas of the accepted prototype for the next holiday season.

You need a firm design choice, materials list, resources, and assembly decision to show Senior Management. You are expected to start production immediately after approval of the Scope and Charter, so the replica can be delivered to your client.

The suppliers. Two Canadian suppliers produce the motor and the control systems. You must use one of them.

  • Metacom Control Systems (MCS), located in Surrey, British Columbia. Your company has worked with MCS in the past and has been very happy with their quality and delivery record. MCS has recently moved a large part of its production to a new plant in Ramos, Mexico, to cut costs.
  • Paragon Technologies, with head offices in Keflavik, Iceland, and a plant in Kingston, Ontario. You have not worked with Paragon before. They are relatively new to the industry and are promoting a new technology that might revolutionize it, but it is not used in many applications yet and is therefore unproven. Paragon’s plant is only 4 hours from your plant in London. Paragon’s employees are negotiating their first labour contract since the Kingston expansion.

The assembly line. Your company needs to source new assembly tooling to make the toy. You have an existing manufacturing line that you could retrofit with the new tooling, but it uses older technology, and production and quality issues have been increasing. A new line would let you introduce new technology to increase production and quality, but your employees and millwright staff would face a learning curve.

The materials. You can source steel and aluminum from local suppliers. Your carbon fibre comes from Fibro Source, a plant in Sherbrooke, Québec. You have used them in the past and have been somewhat satisfied with their quality and delivery on the last two projects you ran. However, they have recently been bought out by a large conglomerate from Metz, France, and are restructuring all their departments. You have heard that there have been many changes to the Canadian management team and that a modernization of the plant, including new process equipment, is underway.

14.5.1 Project Scope

Complete every area with enough detail for your project, in the format used in lecture. The document carries your team name and logo.

  • Scope description: explain every component needed to achieve the project description.
  • Acceptance criteria: state the criteria that decide whether the project succeeded.
  • Validation / testing plan and exclusions: explain how the project is tested and what is needed to pass. Outcomes must be clear and measurable, and the testing methods outlined.
  • Deliverables: define every deliverable, and state clearly which associated items are not deliverables.
  • Project coordination: explain how the project is run. Cover when and where you meet, who creates and keeps documents, how and where information is shared and stored, and how each member’s input is measured and tracked.

14.5.2 Project Charter

Complete every area with enough detail, in the format used in lecture. The document carries your team name and logo.

  • Project description: describe the project accurately. Explain why it matters to your company and what successful completion means for it.
  • Constraints: cover at least time, money, and manpower. Show some thought about other constraints a real project would face.
  • Risks: show you understand the high-level risks that could hurt the project’s success.
  • Project deliverables: define every deliverable, including items such as instruction manuals, software, and hardware where they apply.
  • Project milestones: use actual dates that fall within the assigned timeframe.
  • Project budget: a realistic guess. Use Canadian formatting: a dollar sign first, then commas for thousands, for example $865,000 or $1,112,700.

14.5.3 Work Breakdown Structure

  • Format: use the lecture format, with three levels. The top level may be the project phase. The bottom level is your work packages.
  • Numbering: number the WBS items properly.
  • Completeness: obey the 100 % rule. Work packages are the right size and mutually exclusive.
  • Work packages: at least 20, unless your instructor approves otherwise.
  • Dictionary: include a WBS Dictionary entry for one work package per team member, each assigned to a different member.

14.6.1 Submission

  • Upload a single PDF containing the entire group submission, along with the files in their original format (for example the Excel files).
  • The report starts with the Project Charter, Scope Statement, and WBS from Phase A, corrected using the Phase A feedback.

14.6.2 AON diagram

  • Duration table: a table of activities with durations, predecessors, and successors. Durations should be reasonable and include units (days). The table matches your WBS, and every task has a code.
  • Diagram: a complete AON diagram. Some hammocking is allowed. Include a legend showing which box is ES, LS, and so on. A reasonable number of activities should run in parallel.
  • Calculations: complete and correct forward and backward passes, and the critical path identified.

14.6.3 Risks

  • Identification: at least eight risks (or the number your tutorial instructor sets). Classify each as covered in lecture.
  • Format: each risk has a code, an impact (low, medium, high), a probability (low, medium, high), and an exposure level of green, yellow, or red.
  • Mitigation: a mitigation strategy for every yellow and red risk, categorized as one of the types from lecture.
  • Risk matrix: a properly labelled matrix with each risk’s code in the correct cell. Not every cell needs a risk. Colour the cells to match their exposure level.

14.6.4 Gantt chart

  • Chart: a simple Gantt chart in Excel. Activities match your WBS and AON. Leave every activity at 0 % complete for Phase B.
  • Format: the unit of time is days. Dates can be real dates or start at “Week 1”. Show relationships with arrows. Corner-to-corner tasks can imply Finish-to-Start without an arrow. All relationships can be Finish-to-Start, and they must match your AON.
  • Milestones: mark the end of the activities that match your charter milestones with a diamond. You may also mark the planned milestone dates with a different symbol.
  • Comparison paragraph: compare the milestones in your Charter with the milestone dates in your Gantt chart. They do not need to be the same.

14.7.1 Costs analysis

  • Create a chart that categorizes the costs of your project.
  • Identify the category (or categories) each cost belongs to. A cost can be allocated to more than one category.
  • Use the lecture and your class notes for the category definitions.
  • The example file shows a labour calculation and costs chart, and a materials chart with a contingency reserve. It is only there to help with your preliminary calculations. It is not tied to your project.

Your marks come from three things: the accuracy of your costs, the identification (the logical arrangement) of your costs, and your overall calculations.

14.8 Submission checklist

  1. Group enrolled on FOL, company name and logo chosen.
  2. Correct Project Manager for the phase. No repeats.
  3. Cover sheet on the group report.
  4. Group report completed from the phase templates.
  5. Diagrams and charts readable, with legends where required.
  6. Your evaluation survey for the phase completed on FOL, in the version for your role (PM or Team Member). A missed survey means a grade of 0 for the phase.
  7. Everything uploaded to FOL before the deadline. Remember the 15 % per day late deduction.
